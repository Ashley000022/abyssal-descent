Zone I · Epipelagic · 0–200 m

Sunlight

Nearly everything alive in the ocean lives in this thin, bright lid — the only layer where light is strong enough for photosynthesis. Everything below survives on what sinks out of it.

Sunlight 100 → 1%  ·  up to 30 °C  ·  home to ~90% of marine life

40 m — recreational scuba limit
Specimen 00 · Chelonia mydas

Green sea turtle

0 – 110 M

Ancient navigators of the sunlit zone. They can hold their breath for up to five hours and migrate thousands of kilometres between feeding and nesting grounds, guided by Earth’s magnetic field.

120 m — deepest coral reefs
Zone II · Mesopelagic · 200–1,000 m

Twilight

Only a whisper of blue light reaches here. Every night, billions of animals rise out of this zone to feed near the surface, then sink again before dawn — the largest migration on Earth, twice a day.

Sunlight < 1%  ·  most residents make their own light

Disturb the water — plankton flash where it moves

253 m — deepest free-dive on one breath
Specimen 01 · Argyropelecus

Silver hatchetfish

300 – 600 M

Mirror-sided, with light organs along its belly tuned to match the faint glow above — counter-illumination that erases its silhouette from hunters looking up.

535 m — emperor penguin’s deepest dive
Specimen 02 · Ctenophora

Comb jelly

SURFACE – 700 M

Rows of beating hairs scatter what little light exists into moving rainbows. Not bioluminescence — pure diffraction, running on a body that is 95% water.

1,000 m — the last photon gives up
Zone III · Bathypelagic · 1,000–4,000 m

Midnight

No sunlight has ever touched this water. The only light left is the light life makes for itself — lures, flashes, false eyes, silent alarms. Nine in ten animals here can glow.

Sunlight 0%  ·  ~4 °C  ·  pressure > 100 atmospheres

Specimen 03 · Melanocetus johnsonii

Humpback anglerfish

1,000 – 3,000 M

Her lure is a pocket of glowing bacteria on a modified fin ray. The males are ten times smaller, and when one finds her, he bites on and fuses to her body — for life.

Specimen 04 · Architeuthis dux

Giant squid

300 – 3,000 M

An eye the size of a dinner plate — the largest in the animal kingdom. It isn’t for seeing prey. It’s for spotting the faint shimmer of plankton disturbed by an approaching sperm whale.

2,992 m — deepest dive by any mammal

You are now deeper than any whale has ever gone.

3,688 m — the ocean’s average depth
3,800 m — RMS Titanic rests here
Zone IV · Abyssopelagic · 4,000–6,000 m

The Abyss

Near-freezing black plains that cover more than half the planet. Food is whatever drifts down from the sunlit world — a slow snowfall of the dead, and every few decades, the feast of a fallen whale.

1–2 °C  ·  abyssal plains cover > 50% of Earth’s surface

Specimen 05 · Grimpoteuthis

Dumbo octopus

3,000 – 7,000 M

Swims by flapping the ear-like fins on its head, drifting just above the seafloor. The deepest-living octopus ever recorded — filmed at nearly 7,000 metres.

The snow falling around you is the sunlit world, arriving weeks late.

Zone V · Hadalpelagic · 6,000–10,935 m

Hadal

Named for Hades. It exists only in the trenches, where one tectonic plate slides beneath another. The pressure here would crush a styrofoam cup to the size of a thimble.

Trenches only  ·  pressure approaching 1,100 atmospheres

Specimen 06 · Pseudoliparis

Hadal snailfish

6,000 – 8,336 M

Soft, scaleless, translucent — you can see its organs through its skin. In 2023 one was filmed at 8,336 metres, the deepest any fish has ever been seen.

8,336 m — the last fish. Below this: silence

No fish. No light. Pressure alone, and still — life.

Mariana Trench · full ocean depth

CHALLENGER DEEP

— 10,935 M —

The pressure here is over a thousand times what your body feels at the surface. If Everest were set on this floor, its summit would still be more than two kilometres underwater.

Until 2019, more people had walked on the Moon than had ever stood here. Yet even here, in the dark, amphipods drift past the cameras — pale, curious, alive.

You have descended farther than sunlight ever has.
